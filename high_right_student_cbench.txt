Examiner: "Can you tell me what it means for the mean to be a 'model' of the data?"
Jordan: "It means we're using one number to represent or predict every value in the dataset — the mean is our best single guess for any given data point, even though it won't be exactly right for most of them."
Examiner: "Okay, and what would the 'error' be, in that case?"
Jordan: "The error is the gap between the actual data point and that predicted value — so each point has its own error, equal to the data minus the mean."
 
Examiner: "Why do we use the mean specifically, instead of some other number, as our model?"
Jordan: "Because out of every possible number you could pick, the mean is the one that minimizes the total squared error across all the data points — no other single value fits the data better by that measure."
 
Examiner: "Do you know why we square the errors instead of just adding them up?"
Jordan: "If you just added the raw errors, the positive and negative deviations would cancel out and you'd always get zero, since the mean is the balance point. Squaring keeps everything positive and also penalizes big misses more."
 
Examiner: "Does a large error for a particular data point mean the model is wrong?"
Jordan: "Not necessarily — some error is expected for basically every point, even with the best possible model. Error just reflects natural variation, not that the mean was a bad choice."
